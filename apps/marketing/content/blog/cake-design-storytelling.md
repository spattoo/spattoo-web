Tuba Geçkil made her first sculpted cake for her son's first birthday. She shaped it
like his favourite toy car. He thought it was the real car, and tried to play with it.

She had no formal art training. She grew up in Eskişehir, in central Turkey, with two
parents who painted as amateurs. Today she runs Red Rose Cake, with two shops in
Istanbul, and her hyper-real cakes reached a global audience during the pandemic
lockdowns.

Notice what the first cake was about.

> It was not about the birthday. It was about the boy, and the one object he loved most
> that year. The occasion was only the reason to make it.

That is the shift this article is about.

## 1. The cake is no longer just the celebration

For most of its history, a cake stood for an event. A wedding. A birthday. A festival.
You could swap one cake for another of the same kind and lose nothing, because the cake
was a marker. It said *something is happening today*.

That is changing. More and more, the cake is commissioned around the person inside the
occasion, not the occasion itself. The brief has moved from "a birthday cake for my
daughter" to "something built around what she loves".

It is a small change in wording. It is a large change in what a cake has to do.

## 2. From symbol to personalisation

Cakes have always carried meaning. Ceremonial cakes and sweet breads have stood for
abundance, fertility, marriage, status and hospitality across many cultures. The modern
celebration cake did not arrive from cooking alone. It arrived from ritual.

So the new part is not that cakes mean something. Cakes always meant something.

The new part is *who decides the meaning*.

Before, the meaning came with the occasion, fixed and shared. A wedding cake meant what
wedding cakes meant. Now the customer supplies the meaning, and it is specific to one
person. The cake has gone from a public symbol to a private one.

## 3. When the customer became part of the design

Better tools helped. Fondant, modelling chocolate, edible printing and sharper equipment
widened what a person can physically build out of sugar.

But tools do not explain the change on their own. Skilled decorators could already build
remarkable things fifty years ago.

What changed is expectation. Customers now arrive assuming the cake will be about them.
They ask for a hobby, a job, a pet, a private joke, a place that matters, a film they
have watched thirty times. They bring fragments and expect the fragments to end up in
the design.

The cake becomes something close to a condensed visual biography.

## 4. Pinterest, Instagram and YouTube changed the brief

The easy claim is that social media made cakes more creative. That is not quite right,
and it undersells what happened.

Social media changed what customers know is possible. That changed what they ask for.

**Pinterest** taught people to arrive with a reference board. Instead of "make something
nice", a customer can now specify colour, shape, mood, style and composition before the
first conversation ends. Cake design moved toward visual specification.

**Instagram** turned the cake into a shareable object. A cake now has to work in three
places at once: in the room, in a photograph, and in a feed. That rewards strong
silhouettes, unusual shapes and finishes that photograph well.

It also creates a tension worth naming. If a cake has to perform for an audience that
was never invited, who is it for? A cake built to be meaningful to one person and a cake
built to be impressive to everyone else are not always the same cake. The best designers
are quietly choosing between those two briefs on every job.

**Short video** made the process part of the story. On Reels and Shorts, people watch the
carving, the piping, the painting, the reveal. The finished cake is no longer the whole
product. This turns the cake designer into something of a maker and a broadcaster at the
same time.

**YouTube** spread technique. Sculpting, fondant work, airbrushing, internal structure
and realistic modelling are all teachable now without an apprenticeship. A baker in
Coimbatore can learn a method popularised in London and reinterpret it for a customer in
Coimbatore. That has produced a shared global vocabulary of cake design that did not
exist twenty years ago.

## 5. The cake became a canvas

Five styles show the range of what the cake is now asked to do.

**Vintage and Lambeth.** Joseph Lambeth set out his over-piping method in a 1934 book,
*The Lambeth Method of Cake Decoration and Practical Pastries*. It is built from layer
upon layer of piped scrolls, swags, shells and string work — closer to baroque
architecture than to icing. It peaked around the 1950s, then fondant pushed it aside for
decades. It came back in the 2020s through social media, in bright colours and
with a sense of humour the original never had.

That arc is worth sitting with. A near-forgotten 1930s technique returned because a
video platform made it visible again, and a new generation used it to say something the
1930s never meant by it.

**Sculpted cakes.** Cars, handbags, animals, instruments, buildings. Here the cake stops
carrying decoration and becomes the subject itself.

**Hyper-realistic cakes.** The point is the double take. In 2013 Natalie Sideserf sculpted
a bust of Willie Nelson out of modelling chocolate, bandana and braids included, for a
cake contest in Austin. It won, her brother posted it to Reddit, and it went to the top
of the site. She and her husband both left their jobs and opened Sideserf Cake Studio on
the back of it. The story of a hyper-real cake is surprise: the gap between what you see
and what it is.

**Miniature scenes.** Possibly the clearest storytelling of all. A family house. A
workplace. A childhood street. A travel memory built at ten centimetres tall. The cake
becomes a small three-dimensional scene of something that actually happened.

**Character and fandom cakes.** A character says "this is something I love", and love of
a thing is a real part of how people describe themselves. Fandom is identity in
shorthand.

## 6. So what are people actually saying?

Across all of it, a handful of messages come up again and again.

- **Personality** — through colour, humour, and whether someone wants maximum or almost nothing.
- **Memory** — a childhood home, a family recipe, a school, a holiday.
- **Relationship** — couples, parents and children, friends, the joke only four people get.
- **Work** — the doctor, the architect, the pilot, the person who finally started the business.
- **Obsession** — the sport, the game, the band, the car, the books.
- **Milestone** — graduating, retiring, moving out, opening the shop.
- **Identity** — region, language, culture, faith, and where a person feels they are from.

The useful question about any of these cakes is not *how hard was that to make*. It is
*what is it saying*.

## 7. The Indian cake landscape

India shows this shift clearly.

Research on the Indian cake market describes strong demand for photo cakes, character
cakes for children, tiered wedding cakes and cakes carrying corporate branding.
Personalisation is described as a major selling point rather than an upsell. Instagram
and Pinterest are named directly as the platforms driving which designs people discover
and then request.

One detail from that research matters more than any market number. Customers now expect
to discuss the design itself over WhatsApp or an app, and to get updates while the cake
is being made.

Read that again. The design conversation has become part of the product. The customer is
not ordering a cake. They are commissioning one, and they expect to be present while it
happens.

## 8. The cake designer as storyteller

This is the part that has changed most and is acknowledged least.

A customer rarely arrives with a design. They arrive with fragments. She loves her dog.
They met while travelling. He is obsessed with cricket. It is their first anniversary.

None of those is a cake. Someone has to listen, work out which fragment carries the most
weight, and turn it into something you can look at and understand in a second. That is
interpretation, and it is the actual skill.

It makes the job less like baking and more like design. The person doing it is not only
a maker. They are an interpreter.

Craft also has measurable value to customers. Puratos' Taste Tomorrow research, which
surveys consumers across 50 countries, found that 64% of people globally agree that food
that looks good is tasty too. It also found 24% are willing to pay more for products
that showcase craftsmanship, and 16% would spend extra on visually stunning treats.

Design is not decoration on top of the product. For a growing share of customers, it is
the product.

## 9. Where this goes next

It is tempting to assume the next stage is more spectacular. Taller, more realistic,
harder to believe.

It may be the opposite.

The interesting direction is not more impressive cakes. It is more specific ones. A cake
that only makes complete sense to about six people, because it is built out of things
only those six know, is doing something a technically superior cake cannot.

A customer arrives with a story and no design. The baker has to hold the finished cake
in their head, and the customer has to agree to something they cannot see yet. Almost
everything that goes wrong in custom cake work goes wrong in that gap — rarely in the
baking, which is the part the trade has already mastered.

So the useful question for the next few years is probably not how to make cakes more
impressive. It is how two people who cannot see the same thing can agree on it before
anyone turns on the oven.

The measure stops being *look how creative cakes have become*.

It becomes *look at what people are using cakes to say*.
